## How a split air conditioning system works

Most home air conditioning in the UK is a split system. There is an indoor unit, usually mounted high on a wall, and an outdoor unit outside. The two are joined by insulated copper refrigerant pipes, an electrical cable and a condensate drain that carries away the water the unit removes from the air.

Modern split systems are air-to-air heat pumps. That means the same unit can cool a room in summer and heat it in winter.

Almost every part of the price comes back to that basic setup: how many indoor units, how far apart the indoor and outdoor units are, and what is in the way.

## The main things that affect the price

### 1. How many rooms you want covered

The biggest factor is the number of rooms. One bedroom or home office needs one indoor unit. Covering three bedrooms means three indoor units, either as three separate single splits or as a multi-split, where several indoor units run from one outdoor unit.

A multi-split costs more than a single split, but it can be tidier than having several outdoor units on the outside of your house.

### 2. The length of the pipe run

The pipe run is the distance between the indoor and outdoor units. Every extra metre means more copper pipe, insulation, trunking and time. If the outdoor unit has to go round a corner, along a wall or down to the ground from an upstairs room, the price goes up.

Long runs also need more care at commissioning. Some systems need extra refrigerant added beyond a certain length, which must be done by an F-Gas qualified engineer.

### 3. Back-to-back or not

A back-to-back installation is where the outdoor unit sits on the outside wall directly behind the indoor unit. The pipes go straight through the wall, so it is quick and uses very little pipe. It is usually the cheapest way to fit air conditioning.

If you are flexible about where the indoor unit goes, choosing an outside wall with room for the outdoor unit behind it can save money.

### 4. Your wall type

Every install involves drilling a hole through the wall for the pipes, cable and drain. Cavity brick walls are straightforward. Solid brick, stone, concrete or very thick walls take more time and the right equipment. Rendered or clad walls may need extra care to finish neatly.

### 5. The brand and size of unit

We fit units from Samsung, Fujitsu, Panasonic and Daikin. Each brand has entry-level and premium ranges, with differences in efficiency, noise levels, controls, Wi-Fi apps and air filtration.

Size matters too. A unit is sized by its cooling and heating output, usually given in kilowatts. A small bedroom needs much less than a large open-plan living space or a conservatory. Getting the size right is important: an undersized unit will struggle, and an oversized one costs more for no real benefit.

### 6. The electrics

Air conditioning needs a suitable electrical supply. Many installations need a new dedicated circuit from the consumer unit. In older homes, the consumer unit itself may need upgrading before a new circuit can be added.

Adding a new circuit is notifiable work under building regulations in England. Our electrical and air conditioning work is carried out and certified by NAPIT and REFCOM F-Gas registered engineers.

## Other things that can affect the job

- **Access to the outdoor unit position.** High wall brackets above conservatories or tricky ground may need extra equipment.
- **The condensate drain.** Ideally the water runs away by gravity. Where that is not possible, a small condensate pump is needed.
- **Making good.** Trunking, filling and decorating around the pipework all add to the finish.
- **Planning rules.** In most cases a single domestic outdoor unit on a house does not need planning permission, but there are conditions, and listed buildings, conservation areas and flats can be different. If in doubt, check with your local council or the Planning Portal.

## Why a cheap quote is not always the cheapest

When comparing quotes, check what is included. Ask whether the price includes the electrical circuit and certificate, the pipe length you actually need, the bracket or stand, the condensate route and commissioning. A low headline price can grow once these are added.

It is also worth checking that the installer is F-Gas certified. By law, only certified engineers can work on the refrigerant side of an air conditioning system.
